WordDock

A quiet tool for learning vocabulary and facts, built around focused repetition. Here's how it works.

The idea

Learning sticks when you meet the right item at the right moment — your weakest word, not a random one. WordDock keeps a running score for every word and always builds your next deck from the ones you know least well. No streaks, no penalties, no pressure: just your weakest words, surfaced first, over and over, until they aren't weak any more.

Studying

Tap a card to reveal the answer; tap again while the answer shows to peek back at the question without grading. Mark Good if you recalled it, Again if you slipped. A deck completes after twelve clean passes, and the next deck is drawn from your lowest-scoring words.

The score behind it

Each word carries a running score. At deck completion it rises by one if you never slipped, and falls by one or two if you did. That score does three things at once: it's a tally of how well you know the word, it decides which words come up next (lowest first), and — past a mark of 10, about ten clean recalls — it counts the word as mastered. Nothing about time enters into it: no decay, no "days since review."

Progress

Each domain and each list shows a mastery percentage — how far you are toward knowing every word in it. A list's number climbs quickly (a small set, studied daily); a whole domain's number is the slower, truer measure of how far through the subject you've come. 100% means every word is at mastery or beyond.

Domains and lists

A domain is a subject — a language pair like NL-RU, or a field. Within it, the Full-List is the complete set WordDock provides; your own imported files sit alongside it. Your progress is shared across a whole domain, so studying any list in it builds the same scores.

Your own lists

You can import an .xlsx file to add a list, or export any list to hand it to someone else. A file holds a list's content — its words and cards — never your scores. For an import to be accepted it needs all of the following:

  1. A sheet whose cell A1 is literally ID.
  2. The 25 columns, in order: SEG · ID · ENT · CAT · SUB · TAG · Q1 · A1 · … · Q10 · A10.
  3. At least 10 rows with ID, Q1 and A1 filled.
  4. A filename shaped DOMAIN_name.xlsx — the domain, an underscore, then your name (e.g. NL-RU_holiday.xlsx). The filename is the list's identity.
  5. Within a domain, every entry needs its own ID — never restart numbering. Progress is tracked per ID, so two files both starting at 0001 would make different words share scores. WordDock warns you if it spots this.

Where your progress lives

Your scores stay on your device, in your browser. Nothing leaves it unless you choose to connect another device.

Connecting devices

If you study on more than one device, open Sync, and enter the same key on each. Your progress then travels between them. There's no account, no email, no password — WordDock only ever sees the key, never who you are. Connecting always raises a device to the highest progress across your devices; it never lowers a score another device has already earned.

Keeping a backup file

A backup file is the only true snapshot of your progress. Connecting devices keeps them in step, but if you ever clear the data on all of them, a file is what brings your progress back. Back up from the Backup section whenever you'd like a copy.

Sharing a computer? Progress belongs to the browser, so everyone studying in the same one shares a single set of scores. Give each person their own browser profile — or a different browser — to keep progress apart.
