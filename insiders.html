Section 0draft

What WordDock is, in one page

WordDock is a web-based language learning tool: you study published word lists or build your own. It gives steady progress: small decks of ten words, on your phone or your desktop, your progress remembered. The efficiency gain lets you engage with the language itself: reading, listening, speaking, with the docked words in your memory.

Technically it is two things:

  • a schema: a plain table that any teacher can fill in Excel, with a fixed set of columns, that becomes a list the app can deal cards from;
  • a protocol: the rules for filling that table so the list teaches well, travels across languages, and can be checked by someone else.

Everything below is one of the two.

Section 1draftto complete

The schema: what a list is

  • A list is one space: its rows, its IDs, its pool of scores. Nothing is shared across lists. A list has one name.
  • A row is one word as it is introduced at one place in the curriculum: its entry, its class, its level, and its pairs. The first pair is the word; the pairs after it are the same word, deeper.
  • A view is what the table shows: a level range and a search, per list.
  • A deck is the ten lowest-scoring words of the view; the finish line is all ten clean.
  • The columns: ENT · CAT · SUB · TAG · L · Q1 A1 … Q10 A10. Only Q1 and A1 are required.
  • IDs are stamped at publishing from the row's place and never move again: publish = freeze.

Section 2draft · 14 points

The protocol: how a list is made

The rules, as decided so far. Each has a short reason; a rule without a reason is a habit.

  1. P1

    One row, one word, one place. A word that comes back later in the curriculum is a new row.

    Reason scores hang on rows; a word met twice is learned twice, in two contexts.

  2. P2

    Q1 and A1 are the word; the pairs after it are the same word, deeper. Q2 … A10 are elaboration: a form, a sentence, a pattern.

    Reason the card shows the lemma's edge on pair 2 and later: "same word, deeper", never a new word in disguise.

  3. P3

    The language of CAT, SUB and TAG is the reader's language. The reader is the learner, who reads the question side. In a Dutch-to-Latin list they are Dutch (zn, ww, vrouwelijk); in an English master file they are English. CAT is one value from a closed set per reader language (see section 7); SUB and TAG are free but short.

    Reason a class label the learner cannot read is noise on the card; a label in the target language gives away or confuses.

  4. P4

    A note names a class, never a rule, and never the answer. CAT and SUB are shown on the card before the answer, so neither may give it away: de, f, -la / -le are fine; "o = he / she / it" is not, and neither is a sentence.

    Reason show first, explain later; and the card must not leak.

  5. P5

    The entry is naked, the first answer may carry more. ENT is the most naked dictionary form: infinitive, nominative singular, masculine, no marks. The first answer adds what has to be learned with the word: stress marks (Russian), macrons and principal parts (Latin), an aspect pair.

    Reason the entry is the handle and the search key; the answer is what has to be learned.

  6. P6

    Replace the long dash, never delete it. No long dash (—) in a sentence of WordDock material; Dutch takes a spaced short dash, English a comma, a colon or a full stop; in Russian the dash is grammar and stays as a spaced short dash.

    Reason house style; and in Russian the dash carries meaning.

  7. P7

    The half-pyramid for lesson lists. A sentence in unit n uses only words and patterns of units up to n.

    Reason a sentence the learner cannot yet read teaches nothing; the first deck plays in order.

  8. P8

    Short, concrete, one idea. A pair's sentence is 2 to 6 words, says one thing, and the second sentence puts the word in another position. One-syllable words first; long words and exceptions in later units.

    Reason the mechanics of memory; see the hub charter, rule 0.

  9. P9

    Easy to translate. In a master that every language translates, beginner words stay close to concepts every language has a word for: tea, house, friend, good. A place name is fine (Moscow, Kinshasa). A word that needs a sentence to explain in another language is not, at least not at the start.

    Reason every language has to say the same line, so a beginner line should cost the translator no explaining.

  10. P10

    Provenance and copyright are transparent. Every published list says who made it, from what, and under which licence; a list made from a book needs the rights settled before it is published. The metadata will live in the sheet itself, on its first sheet (to design).

    Reason the shelf is public; a learner picks a list by trusting its origin.

  11. P11

    Publish = freeze. A published row keeps its ID and its meaning forever; a correction of the text is allowed, a change of meaning is a new row at the end. Before the first publish on the live site, anything may move.

    Reason the learner's scores hang on the ID.

  12. P12

    A reviewer, not a rule, promotes a list. Machine-made material is beta until one person who knows the language has read it.

    Reason the bottleneck is people, and that is the right bottleneck.

  13. P13

    One layer at a time. Complexity comes gradually and openly: first the masculine singular, later the plural, never mixed with gender in one cue. A form that is its own dictionary word is its own row: vriend and vriendin are two words, as что the conjunction and что the question word are two, even when English has one word (friend) for both. Adjectives are the exception: goed may show goede in a later pair. Fixed phrases (goedendag) are their own row.

    Reason a learner who meets two layers at once learns neither; and "two words that look alike" is itself a lesson.

  14. P14

    A row's identity is ENT · CAT · SUB, not ENT alone. Two entries that look alike are two words when their class differs: Russian нет the particle (nee) and нет the predicative (er is geen) are two rows; so are что and что. Gender splits a word only where the language has two words: German Kollege and Kollegin are two rows, Dutch collega is one word (SUB m. / v.) and is repeated in the feminine row, so a Dutch-German pair still plays Kollegin. A form of the same lemma is not a new word: Dutch ze is the unstressed zij, so it stays inside zij's row or comes in a later layer.

    Reason the learner's score hangs on the row; one row per real word, no more and no fewer.

Section 3draft

Kinds of lists, and their names

  • My list: an Excel sheet added on one device; private; the file name is the list name.
  • WD list: published on the site; one JSON file plus one name on the app's list.
  • Hub pair: made in the browser from two language files; its name is the two codes, NL-TR.
  • The naming law: two-code names belong to the hub; a curated pair list carries a third segment that says its angle: NL-LA-PHOENIX, NL-RU-S, NL-IT-LEARNAMO. Many lists per pair are welcome; the app orders nothing across them.
  • Tiers: beta (machine-made, unchecked) · published (read by one reviewer) · curated (a named curator, the full protocol).
  • The provenance line on the shelf: source · curator · tier · level span. to build

Section 8draft

Code logic: the engine

What the app does with a list, as the code does it (index.html v0.30). For the curious, and for whoever checks a build.

8.1 One file

The app is one HTML file. A published list is one JSON file under lists/. A My list is read from its Excel file in the browser and kept on the device. On the live site a service worker keeps a copy for offline use, and a small sync worker keeps the scores; a copy of the app on another address (the beta) has neither.

8.2 Reading a sheet

The columns are found by their names, in any order, on the first sheet that has Q1 and A1. A row needs Q1 and A1; the rest may be empty; an empty ENT takes A1. Up to ten pairs per row. In a My list the ID is the row's place (0001, 0002 …); in a published list the ID is in the file.

8.3 What the device stores

Per list one pool of scores, wd_rs_<NAME>: ID to number. Per list the view (level range and search). The list last studied, the deck in progress, the My lists, and the sync key and stamps. Nothing else; no account.

8.4 The view

The table shows the rows that pass two filters: the level range (A1, A1.3, A1-A2, -B1, B1-; a list without levels ignores it) and the search. A search that is exactly a CAT, SUB or TAG value filters on that value; any other text searches the whole row, with case and accents folded ("amicus" finds "amīcus"). Rows per page: as many as fit the screen, never a scrollbar.

8.5 The deck

Study takes the visible rows, sorts them by score (lowest first) and then by ID, and takes the first ten. So a fresh list plays in order, and later the weakest come first. Each card in the deck remembers: its row, a finger on the current pair, whether this pass is dirty, whether it owes a redemption pass, how many times it was dirty, and whether it is clean.

8.6 The pipe

The deck is a queue; the card at the front is the one shown. Tap: the answer; tap again: a peek at the question. Then Again or Good.

  • Again: the card stays at the front; the pass is now dirty (counted once per pass); the card is not clean.
  • Good: on to the next pair of the same card. After the last pair:
    • if the pass was dirty: the card owes a redemption pass; it stays at the front and plays again from pair 1;
    • if that was the redemption pass: the card goes to position 3, still not counted;
    • otherwise: the card is clean, goes to the back, and is counted.
  • The counter at the top is "clean cards / cards in the deck". Done when every card is clean.

8.7 The score

Only at the end of a deck does a score change: +1 for a card that was never dirty, −1 for a card dirty once, −2 for a card dirty more than once. Nothing is written during a deck. The score has no clock: it says how often a word came back clean, not when it is due.

8.8 Resume

The deck in progress is saved after every grade. On reopening, the same deck comes back if it is the same list and the same view (the signature is the IDs of the visible rows); otherwise a new deck.

8.9 Sync

At the end of a deck: pull, merge, push. Merge, per ID: present on both sides, the higher score wins; present on one side, taken. The cloud stores a key and pools of numbers, never who you are.

8.10 Names

A list has one name: capitals, digits, hyphens. It names the file, the pool, the view, the heading. A My list with the name of a published list gets -OWN; the published list keeps the name.

Section 9proposal

A paper, one day

A table of contents for an academic paper on the principles, and a first reference list. Working title: Show first, explain later: a schema and a protocol for shareable word lists. Alternative: WordDock: weakest-first vocabulary practice on a plain table.

Table of contents

  1. Introduction: the bottleneck is the material, not the schedule. Most tools optimise when a card returns; few say what a card may contain. The question: can a plain table, with rules, be the unit of exchange between teachers?
  2. The schema: a list as a minimal data structure. Rows, pairs, levels, IDs; one space per list; comparison with flashcard formats (Anki notes, CSV decks).
  3. The protocol: the fourteen rules and their grounding (section 2 of this page).
  4. The engine: a deck of ten, dirty passes, redemption, the score at completion (section 8). What it deliberately does not do: no timing model, no due dates.
  5. Didactic principles: show first, explain later; the half-pyramid (i+1, the zone of proximal development); one-syllable words first; re-entry rows (the spiral curriculum).
  6. Lexicon: lemma and form; "same word, deeper"; collocations and fixed expressions as rows; frequency and coverage.
  7. Syntax and grammar inside a word list: a class label, not a rule; the note on the card; focus on form without explanation; the Russian dash as grammar.
  8. Linguistic diversity: the hub, one file per language; masters that are easy to translate; stress marks and macrons; what one story in eight languages reveals (Mister X).
  9. Learning psychology: retrieval practice, spacing, desirable difficulties, cognitive load; the counter as feedback; why the score is slow.
  10. Social design: cathedral and bazaar; tiers; provenance and licences; the reviewer as the right bottleneck.
  11. Evaluation: what to measure (the pool over time, re-entry rows, a reviewer's corrections); a first study design with the NL-LA list.
  12. Limitations and future work.

Section 1024 Oct

Decisions, and why

The why behind the current state, only what still holds. The full record, with what each decision replaced, is in the project (v27/decisions.md for October 1 to 5, v27/build.md after that).

  • The list is the space. Its rows, its IDs, its pool. An earlier design had a container above the list that shared one pool among several files; it dissolved once a part of a list became a view instead of a file, because a view never asks "same scores or new ones?".
  • One name, no titles. The name is the file name, the pool key, the heading, the pill. Titles were built once and withdrawn: two things to keep in step for no gain.
  • The level column is L, band and unit. It replaced an integer scheme in hundreds and, before that, a letter-and-digit segment code; CEFR bands are what teachers already speak.
  • IDs are stamped at publishing, never typed. The crafter's handle is the entry, the class and the level; the number is the machine's.
  • Publish = freeze, and grow at the end. Scores hang on IDs; a moved row would move a learner's score to another word.
  • Headers by name, one name per column, no aliases. Names from earlier trials were accepted silently for a while and misled a reader; now an unknown header is named back to the crafter and nothing older is read.
  • Required: Q1 and A1; everything else optional. The smallest list is two words.
  • Study deals from the view. Filter first, then Study: the ten weakest of what you see, into the list's one pool.
  • Four pages in a ring, one door each, one green accent. Five arrangements of the doors were tried in two days in October; what survived is the one where every page has exactly one way out, at the bottom, and the way into studying is the one green pill. Orange and a Van Eyck blue were tried live and dropped: green reads as movement and growth.
  • The list-page pill says "Study N". N is the size of the selection; the deck's counter belongs to the card, not to the list page.
  • All lists shows the RS sum, not a percentage. The plain sum is honest and needs no explanation; the list being studied comes first.
  • English interface only. Forty strings, forever, in every language, for no learner's benefit; content can be in any language.
  • SRC is a column of the format, optional. Provenance per row survives sorting, filtering and merging; an empty SRC is the crafter's own.
  • Two lists for two ways of asking. Recognition and production are two skills; they get two pools.
  • Names: hard where the software needs it, advice where it doesn't.
  • Nobody's full name in anything published. Initials.
